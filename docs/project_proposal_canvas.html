First they write a proposal that convinces someone (a professor, a company, a grant committee) that they have found a good problem, understand how hard it is, know what others have already tried, and have a realistic plan to attack it. Then they document their progress so others can see the work is really happening.

This semester you will do the same thing. Your proposal is your "grant request," and your portfolio is your weekly progress report to the people funding your work.

1. Overview & Requirements

Each student will design, research, build, and test an independent project. The project must have something you can build or implement (for example a program, app, game, model, device, or simulation) and something you can measure to decide whether it worked.

Your written proposal should include enough detail to convince the reader that:

  • you've found a good problem that someone would benefit from solving;
  • you understand how hard it is, and you've scoped it so it can be finished by December 20;
  • you know what others have already tried, and you have cited real sources;
  • you've mapped out a step-by-step plan, including a backup plan for the hardest step; and
  • you know which experiments or tests you will run to measure success.
Requirement Details
Length 4–6 pages (about 1,500–2,500 words), not counting the cover page and References.
Format Typed, 11–12 pt font, 1.15–1.5 line spacing, with headings that match the section names below. Tables, diagrams, and screenshots are encouraged. Submit as a PDF.
Sources At least 5 credible sources, cited in APA 7th edition format. See the Literature Review Guide for which kinds of sources are required.
Due Full proposal due Sunday, November 15 at 11:59 PM (Portfolio Deliverable 3). Smaller pieces are due earlier; see the timeline.
Use of AI tools & outside help If you use AI tools, tutorials, or someone else's code, you must say so. List the tool or source, what you used it for, and what you changed. Undisclosed use is treated as plagiarism. All writing in the proposal must be your own.

3. Proposal Structure (Section by Section)

Your proposal must use the sections below, in this order, with these headings. Each section lists what to include, the questions you must answer, and a suggested length. The suggested lengths add up to about 4–6 pages.

Tip: Write the Abstract last. It is a summary of everything else, so it's much easier to write once the other sections are done.

Cover Page (1 page, not counted toward length)

  • Project title: specific and descriptive. "Adaptive Fraction Quiz Game for 6th Graders" is better than "My Game."
  • Your name ("Principal Investigator"), course, teacher, and date
  • A one-sentence pitch: "I will build ___ for ___ so that ___."

Section 1: Abstract (150–200 words)

A single paragraph summary of the whole proposal: the problem, why it matters, your approach, how you'll measure success, and what you expect to deliver by December 20. A busy reader should be able to read only this paragraph and understand your project.

Section 2: Goal & Statement of Need (½ page)

You must answer
  • What am I going to do? State the finished product in one or two concrete sentences.
  • Who would benefit? Name a specific audience or user, such as students in an intro Python class, a local food bank, or players of a certain game.
  • What is the need? What problem does this audience have today, and what evidence shows that it is a real problem?
Also include 2–4 Specific Aims, written as a numbered list of measurable objectives. Example: "Aim 1: Generate at least 200 unique, correctly-answered fraction problems across 4 difficulty levels."

Section 3: Problem Statement (½–1 page)

You must answer
  • Why is it hard? Name the technical challenges, such as algorithms you don't know yet, messy data, hardware limits, user-interface design, or performance.
  • How hard is it? Compare it to things you've done before. What is new to you? Estimate the hours it will take.
  • Scope: What is in the project, and what is deliberately out? Saying what you will not do shows that you understand the difficulty.
  • Constraints: time (8 weeks), tools, data, hardware, and skills.

Section 4: Background & Literature Review (Previous Work) (1–1½ pages)

You must answer
  • What have others tried? Describe at least 5 sources: existing products, projects, research, or techniques.
  • What worked for them, and what didn't work, or what is still missing?
  • What is the gap your project fills, or what is your new twist?
  • Which ideas, tools, or methods will you borrow, and from whom?
Important This is not a list of summaries. Organize it by theme and compare the sources with each other. See the Literature Review Guide.

Section 5: Approach (½ page)

You must answer
  • What approach am I going to try? Give the language, libraries, platform, algorithms, hardware, and overall design.
  • Why do I think it will work well? Connect your reasons to your literature review. For example: "Source 3 showed that spaced repetition improves recall, so I will…"
  • Include a simple diagram (flowchart, system diagram, or wireframe) of how the pieces fit together.

Section 6: Methodology, Task List & Risk Plan (1 page)

You must answer
  • What steps are required? A numbered task list with 10–20 tasks, each with an estimated number of hours.
  • Which of these steps is particularly hard? Identify the 1–2 riskiest tasks and explain why.
  • What will I do if the hard steps don't work out? Describe a concrete Plan B, such as a simpler algorithm, a smaller feature set, or an existing library, and the date by which you'll decide to switch.

Include a risk table like this one in your proposal:

Hard Step / Risk Why It's Risky Plan B Decision Date
Example: Generating wrong answer choices that look believable Wrong answers might accidentally equal the right answer, or be obviously silly Use a fixed list of common-mistake rules (e.g., adding numerators and denominators) and filter out duplicates Dec 6 (Midpoint Review)

Section 7: Evaluation Plan & Metrics (½–1 page)

You must answer
  • How will I measure success? Use numbers wherever possible, such as accuracy, speed, number of features working, user ratings, or error rate.
  • Which experiments will I run? Describe each test: what you'll do, who or what is tested, how many trials, and what data you'll record.
  • Define three levels of success:
    • Minimum: what you will definitely deliver
    • Target: what you expect to deliver
    • Stretch: what you'll attempt if things go well

Section 8: Timeline & Deliverables (½ page, a table is fine)

Copy the 8-week schedule from Section 7 of this handout and fill in your own project-specific milestones for each week. For example, write "Week 5: quiz loop runs end-to-end with 3 question types" instead of just "prototype." Your teacher will use this table to check your weekly portfolio uploads, so make it realistic.

Section 9: Resources Needed ("Budget") (¼ page)

Real grant proposals ask for money. You're asking for resources. List everything you need:

  • Software: languages, libraries, editors, accounts
  • Hardware: computers, sensors, devices
  • Data: datasets, question banks, images, and where they come from
  • People: testers, mentors, or experts you'll ask for feedback
  • Time: estimated total hours and hours per week

Mark anything you don't have yet and say how you'll get it.

Section 10: Summary & Expected Learning (¼–½ page)

  • What will I learn by doing this project? Name specific technical skills (for example, recursion, APIs, or data visualization) and research or process skills.
  • Why is this project worth the time? Restate its value to the people who benefit.

References (separate page, not counted toward length)

List every source in APA 7th edition format, alphabetized by author. Every reference must be cited at least once in the text, for example (Smith, 2023), and every in-text citation must appear in the list.

4. Guide to Writing Your Literature Review

A literature review answers the question "What have others tried?" It shows you're not reinventing the wheel, and it gives you ideas you can build on. You will build it in two steps: an annotated bibliography (D2, due Nov 8), which then becomes the Literature Review section of your proposal (D3, due Nov 15).

Required Source Mix (minimum 5 sources)

Source Type Minimum Examples & Where to Look
Research or technical article 1 Google Scholar, your school library databases, ACM/IEEE magazines, university research pages, conference papers. Reading the abstract, introduction, and conclusion is enough.
Existing product or project you tried hands-on 2 Apps, games, websites, or open-source GitHub projects that solve a similar problem. Actually use them and note their strengths and weaknesses.
Official documentation or tutorial 1 Docs for the language, library, or hardware you will use (e.g., docs.python.org, Flask docs, Arduino reference).
Expert or credible explanatory source 1 Reputable news or science journalism, textbooks, talks by practitioners, or an interview with an expert (such as a teacher, engineer, or potential user).

Wikipedia is a fine place to start, but it does not count as one of your 5 sources. Follow its references to the original sources instead.

Is This Source Credible? Quick Check

  • Author: Who wrote it? Do they have expertise?
  • Date: Is it recent enough? For technology, aim for the last 5–10 years unless it's a classic.
  • Purpose: Is it trying to inform, or to sell you something?
  • Evidence: Does it show data, results, or working code?

Annotated Bibliography Entry Format (for D2)

For each source, write the APA citation followed by a 4–6 sentence annotation that answers:

  1. Summary: What did they do or build, and what did they find?
  2. Evaluation: How credible and useful is it? What are its limitations?
  3. Connection: How will this source change or support my project?

Synthesis Matrix (for D2)

Before you write the review, organize your sources in a matrix so you can see patterns across sources. Choose 3–4 themes that fit your project.

Source Theme 1: e.g., How they solved it Theme 2: e.g., How they measured success Theme 3: e.g., Limitations / gaps
Source A (Author, Year)   
Source B (Author, Year)   
…   

Turning the Matrix into the Literature Review (for D3)

  • Write one paragraph per theme (one column of your matrix), not one paragraph per source.
  • Use comparison language: "Both X and Y use…, but only Y…" or "Unlike earlier tools, Z…"
  • End with a gap paragraph: "None of these projects…, which is what my project will address by…"

5. Don't Be Vague: Weak vs. Strong Writing

The most common reason a proposal loses points is vague writing. A reader should be able to picture exactly what you'll build and exactly how you'll test it.

Section Vague (weak) Specific (strong)
Goal "I'm going to make a game that helps people learn." "I will build a browser-based Python quiz game that generates randomized fraction problems for 6th graders and tracks each player's accuracy on each skill."
Problem "It might be kind of hard." "The hardest part is generating wrong answer choices that are believable but never accidentally correct. I have never written a problem generator, so I estimate 8–10 hours for this step alone."
Previous Work "There are lots of quiz apps online." "Kahoot and Blooket rely on teacher-written questions, so a question set runs out quickly (Source 2, Source 3). Research on adaptive practice shows… (Source 1). My game will generate problems automatically so it never repeats."
Metrics "I'll test it to see if it works." "I'll run automated tests on 1,000 generated problems (target: 100% correct answer keys, 0 duplicate choices) and have 5 classmates play 10 rounds each (target: average rating of 4 out of 5 or higher for 'fun')."
Plan B "If it doesn't work I'll try something else." "If the generator isn't reliable by Dec 6, I'll switch to a hand-written bank of 100 problems and focus the project on the adaptive difficulty system instead."

6. Your Project Portfolio

Your portfolio is the public record of your work. It is where you upload proof of progress every week. A reader should be able to scroll through it and watch your project grow from an idea into a finished product.

Setting It Up (Deliverable 1)

  • Create your portfolio on the platform your teacher has assigned (for example, a Google Site, Canvas ePortfolio, or GitHub repository with a README).
  • Make a Home page with your project title, your one-sentence pitch, and a link to each deliverable.
  • Make one page or section per deliverable, named D1 - Pitch, D2 - Annotated Bibliography, and so on.
  • Make sure your teacher can view it. Test the link in a private or incognito browser window.

What Counts as "Proof of Progress"?

Telling isn't proof. Showing is. Every deliverable must include evidence such as:

  • Screenshots of your program running
  • Short screen recordings or demo videos (1–3 min)
  • Links to code commits, with dates
  • Test output and error messages you solved
  • Photos of sketches, wireframes, and whiteboard plans
  • Data tables, charts, and survey results
  • Feedback you received and how you responded to it
  • An updated task list with completed items checked off

Weekly Progress Log (required with every deliverable D2–D8)

Along with each week's specific deliverable, add a short progress log entry using this template:
